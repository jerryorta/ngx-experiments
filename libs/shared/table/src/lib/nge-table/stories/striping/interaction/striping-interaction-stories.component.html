NgeTable — Zebra Striping (Interaction)

Striping is one boolean, and it would be a one-line feature if a stripe could be taken from the DOM. It cannot. Examples 2 and 3 are the two that decide whether the implementation is right, and neither can be checked from a screenshot: a :nth-child stripe looks perfect until you scroll, and a stripe keyed on the engine's row.index looks perfect until you sort. Examples 4 to 7 check the other half — that every mark still outranks the stripe.

1. The flag, on and off

enableStriping defaults to false, and with it off the table must render exactly as it did before the feature existed. Toggle the enableStriping control in the Storybook panel: nothing moves except the alternate rows' surface.

2. A stripe survives a virtualized scroll

Ten thousand rows, of which about thirty are in the DOM at any moment. Angular's @for reorders those nodes as the window slides, so a row's position among its rendered siblings changes constantly — which is exactly what :nth-child reads. Under a :nth-child stripe the bands would stand still while the rows travelled through them.

Scroll slowly, a row at a time, and follow one row. Its stripe goes with it. Then scroll to the bottom and back: the pattern is unchanged, because parity comes from the row's place in the processed row model rather than from the DOM.

3. A sort re-stripes the rows

Click the Name header. The rows reorder and the banding stays regular, because parity is re-cut from the processed row model. TanStack's own row.index is the position in config.data and is carried through the sorted model unchanged — a stripe keyed on it would scatter into an arbitrary pattern here, which is the failure that survives a scroll and still ships broken.

4. Hover outranks the stripe

Run the pointer down the table. Every row lifts by the same amount. An alternate row is neither "already hovered" nor immune to it, because the stripe declares no background of its own — it swaps the value the row's background already resolves through, so the hover rule keeps winning on specificity exactly as it did before striping existed.

5. Selection outranks the stripe

The case a naive implementation gets wrong, and it shows on alternate rows only. Select an odd row and an even one. Both must read as equally selected — a stripe that out-ranked selection would leave every other selected row looking untouched, which is easy to miss and hard to diagnose.

6. One band across three lanes

The pinned lanes are position: sticky and opaque by requirement, so they scroll over the center lane and would otherwise hide the row's own surface. They read the same resolved property by inheritance.

Scroll horizontally. A striped row stays one continuous band across the pinned-left, center and pinned-right seams rather than breaking into three.

7. All of it at once

Ten thousand rows — striped, virtualized, pinned and selectable together. Every axis the stripe has to survive, composed into one table.

Select a few rows near the top, scroll to the bottom and back, then scroll sideways. The marks stay on their records, the stripes stay on their rows, and the bands stay unbroken.
